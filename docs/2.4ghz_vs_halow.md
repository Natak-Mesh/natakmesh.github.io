# 2.4 GHz vs WiFi HaLow: Why We Chose 2.4 GHz for the Mesh

## Why This Document Exists

We did not pick 2.4 GHz because it was easy or familiar. We spent most of a year
building on WiFi HaLow (802.11ah) before we shipped anything, and we moved to a
2.4 GHz mesh only after the HaLow builds failed to earn their keep in the field.

The most common piece of feedback we get is some version of "HaLow would be
better, it's sub-GHz, it goes further." It is a reasonable instinct and it is
mostly wrong for this application. This document lays out the reasoning with the
math shown, so it can be checked rather than taken on faith.

Scope: this is strictly a comparison of the radio link **between two mesh
nodes**. Client device support, regulatory questions, and coexistence with other
onboard radios are separate topics and are not covered here.

## The Assumption Being Challenged

> "900 MHz has better propagation than 2.4 GHz, so a HaLow mesh will have more
> range than a 2.4 GHz mesh."

The first half of that sentence is true in free space. The second half does not
follow, for three independent reasons:

1. At the antenna heights people actually operate at, the propagation advantage
   of 900 MHz disappears completely.
2. A narrow channel does not create capacity. It buys link margin at a very poor
   exchange rate.
3. Once the honest link budget is drawn with identical transmit hardware, HaLow's
   *fastest* rate quits before 2.4 GHz's *slowest* rate does.

The rest of this document works through each one, then states where HaLow
genuinely does win (section 8) and answers the five strongest counter-arguments
against our position (section 9).

## Assumptions Used Throughout

Every number below comes from these inputs. They are stated up front so the
arithmetic can be reproduced or disputed.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Antenna height, both nodes | 4 ft (1.22 m) — how a portable node is actually carried |
| Conducted output power, both radios | 27 dBm |
| Antenna gain, both radios | 5 dBi omnidirectional |
| EIRP, both radios | 32 dBm |
| Receiver noise figure | 5 dB |
| Implementation margin over theoretical SNR | 3 dB |
| 2.4 GHz channel | 20 MHz (HT20) |
| HaLow channel | 2 MHz primary, 1 MHz noted where relevant |
| Terrain | Flat, clear line of sight, no foliage unless stated |

Both radios are given **identical transmit power and identical antenna gain**.
This matters: a lot of published HaLow comparisons quietly give the sub-GHz radio
a link-budget advantage it does not have, or quietly compare a 27 dBm WiFi card
against a 20 dBm HaLow module. Here they start even.

## 1. At Man-Portable Height, the Frequency Advantage Is Zero

In free space, 900 MHz enjoys about 8.5 dB less path loss than 2.4 GHz at the
same distance. That advantage is real, and it is also almost entirely irrelevant
to a node sitting on a pack or a tripod at chest height.

Close to the ground, the signal reaches the far node by two paths: the direct one
and a ground reflection. Past a certain distance the reflected copy arrives out
of phase and cancels a large part of the direct signal. Loss stops following free
space at 20 dB/decade and starts following the two-ray plane-earth model at
40 dB/decade. The distance where that transition happens is:

```
d_c = 4 * h1 * h2 / λ
```

At 4 ft on both ends:

| Band | Wavelength | Breakpoint distance |
|---|---|---|
| 2.4 GHz | 0.125 m | **47.6 m** |
| 900 MHz | 0.333 m | **17.8 m** |

Note which way that cuts. The longer wavelength reaches its breakpoint *sooner*.
900 MHz starts paying the 40 dB/decade penalty at 18 m while 2.4 GHz is still
coasting on free-space loss out to 48 m. The sub-GHz radio spends its entire
propagation advantage inside that window.

Past the breakpoint, the two-ray model is:

```
L = 40*log10(d) - 20*log10(h1 * h2)
```

**There is no wavelength term in that equation.** Path loss past the breakpoint
is frequency-independent. Both bands land on the same number:

| Distance | 2.4 GHz two-ray loss | 900 MHz two-ray loss | Advantage to HaLow |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 m | 88.6 dB | 88.6 dB | **0.0 dB** |
| 500 m | 104.5 dB | 104.5 dB | **0.0 dB** |
| 1,000 m | 116.6 dB | 116.6 dB | **0.0 dB** |
| 1,500 m | 123.6 dB | 123.6 dB | **0.0 dB** |

This is the single most misunderstood point in the whole comparison. At 4 ft
antenna height over open ground, past about 50 m, **900 MHz has no propagation
advantage over 2.4 GHz at all.** Whatever range advantage HaLow has in this
scenario has to come from somewhere else — and it does, but not from here.

Two caveats, so this is not overstated. The two-ray model assumes a flat
reflecting surface and idealised reflection; real ground is rough, and rough
ground scatters rather than reflects cleanly, which softens the cancellation
somewhat — and it softens it *more* at 2.4 GHz, since surface roughness is
measured relative to wavelength. Real behaviour therefore sits between the
free-space and two-ray curves rather than exactly on the 40 dB/decade line. But
the direction of the argument is unchanged: raise the antennas and the 8.5 dB
free-space advantage reappears; keep them at chest height and most or all of it
is consumed by ground effects that 900 MHz enters sooner.

## 2. Where HaLow's Range Actually Comes From: Narrow Channels

HaLow's real advantage is not the frequency, it is the channel width. Thermal
noise scales with bandwidth:

```
Noise floor = -174 dBm/Hz + 10*log10(Bandwidth) + Noise Figure
```

| Channel | Noise floor (NF = 5 dB) |
|---|---|
| 20 MHz (2.4 GHz HT20) | −96 dBm |
| 2 MHz (HaLow) | −106 dBm |
| 1 MHz (HaLow) | −109 dBm |

A 2 MHz channel sits 10 dB below a 20 MHz channel. That 10 dB is genuine, and it
is the entire basis of HaLow's range claim. The question is what it costs.

## 3. Narrow Channels Do Not Create Capacity

Shannon-Hartley:

```
C = B * log2(1 + SNR)
```

Capacity is **linear in bandwidth** and **logarithmic in SNR**. Narrowing the
channel by 10× to gain 10 dB of SNR is therefore a bad trade — you pay a factor
of ten to be repaid a logarithm.

Holding received power fixed at −90 dBm and varying only channel width:

| Channel | Noise floor | SNR | Shannon capacity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 MHz | −96 dBm | 6 dB | **46.3 Mbps** |
| 8 MHz | −100 dBm | 10 dB | 27.6 Mbps |
| 2 MHz | −106 dBm | 16 dB | **10.7 Mbps** |
| 1 MHz | −109 dBm | 19 dB | 6.3 Mbps |

Same signal, same conditions, same instant. The wide channel carries more than
four times the traffic of the 2 MHz channel *while both are receiving identical
power*. Narrowing the channel never produces throughput. What it produces is the
ability to keep demodulating something after the wide channel has fallen off its
sensitivity cliff — at a rate the narrow channel was always limited to anyway.

## 4. The Link Budget with Identical Hardware

With 32 dBm EIRP on both ends, a 5 dB noise figure, and 3 dB of implementation
margin over theoretical SNR requirements:

| Link | PHY rate | Sensitivity | Max path loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.4 GHz HT20, MCS3 (16-QAM 1/2) | 26 Mbps | −82 dBm | 114 dB |
| 2.4 GHz HT20, MCS0 (BPSK 1/2) | 6.5 Mbps | −88 dBm | 120 dB |
| HaLow 2 MHz, MCS7 (64-QAM 5/6) | 6.5 Mbps | −81 dBm | 113 dB |
| HaLow 2 MHz, MCS0 (BPSK 1/2) | 0.65 Mbps | −98 dBm | 130 dB |
| HaLow 1 MHz, MCS0 (BPSK 1/2) | 0.30 Mbps | −101 dBm | 133 dB |

Two things fall out of this table.

**HaLow's top speed quits before 2.4 GHz's bottom speed.** Both rows marked
6.5 Mbps carry the same PHY rate. HaLow needs −81 dBm to hold it; 2.4 GHz holds
it down to −88 dBm. HaLow's *best case* runs out of margin 7 dB — about 1.5× in
distance — **closer in** than the point where 2.4 GHz drops to its *worst case*.
Anywhere HaLow is still moving video-class traffic, 2.4 GHz is moving four times
more.

**HaLow's extra reach exists only at telemetry rates.** The 130 dB and 133 dB
figures are real, and they are 10–13 dB better than 2.4 GHz can manage. But they
are attached to 0.65 and 0.30 Mbps PHY rates, which after 802.11 overhead land
somewhere around 0.2–0.4 Mbps of actual goodput. That is a position-and-chat
pipe, not a network.

## 5. Turning the Link Budget into Distance

Solving the two-ray equation for distance at 4 ft antenna height on both ends:

| Link | PHY rate | Predicted range at 4 ft |
|---|---|---|
| HaLow 2 MHz, MCS7 | 6.5 Mbps | 815 m |
| 2.4 GHz HT20, MCS3 | 26 Mbps | 863 m |
| 2.4 GHz HT20, MCS0 | 6.5 Mbps | 1,219 m |
| HaLow 2 MHz, MCS0 | 0.65 Mbps | 2,168 m |
| HaLow 1 MHz, MCS0 | 0.30 Mbps | 2,577 m |

**Sanity check against our own field data.** Our open-field range test ran two
nodes at approximately 4 ft elevation with stock antennas. Throughput fell below
1 Mbps at 800 m, the link held at 900 m, and it was gone between 900 m and 1 km.
The model above predicts the 2.4 GHz link expiring at 1,219 m. For a clean-earth
model with no terrain, foliage, or antenna pattern effects, agreement inside a
few hundred metres is about as good as this class of calculation gets, and it
errs optimistic in exactly the way you would expect.

Read the table in order and the trade is obvious. Sorted by range, HaLow appears
at the top and the bottom: its high-rate mode is the *shortest*-range link in the
comparison, and its long-range mode buys roughly 1.8× more distance than 2.4 GHz
while carrying about a tenth of the traffic.

## 6. Elevation Beats Frequency by an Order of Magnitude

The `- 20*log10(h1 * h2)` term is where the real leverage is. Holding the budget
at 120 dB and varying only antenna height:

| Antenna height (both ends) | Two-ray loss at 1 km | Range at 120 dB budget |
|---|---|---|
| 1.22 m (4 ft, on the body) | 116.5 dB | 1,220 m |
| 2 m | 108.0 dB | 2,000 m |
| 3 m | 100.9 dB | 3,000 m |
| 5 m | 92.0 dB | 5,000 m |
| 10 m (mast, rooftop, vehicle) | 80.0 dB | 10,000 m |
| 15 m | 73.0 dB | 15,000 m |

Going from 4 ft to 10 m removes **36 dB** of path loss and multiplies range by
about eight. Switching from 2.4 GHz to 900 MHz at 4 ft removes **0 dB** and
multiplies range by 1.78 — and only at telemetry rates.

This is not a theoretical result. Our maximum-range test put a node roughly 48 m
above a lake and linked the **same 2.4 GHz hardware at 5.5 km**, with throughput
around 4 Mbps between 2 and 2.5 km. Same radios, same stock antennas, same
firmware that struggles to hold 900 m at chest height.

The conclusion we draw from this, and the reason the mesh is built the way it is:
**range is an antenna-height problem, not a frequency problem.** A team that puts
one node on a mast, a rooftop, a vehicle, or a piece of high ground gains more
than any band change can deliver, and keeps the throughput.

## 7. What a Mesh Does to a Low Ceiling

Everything above concerns a single hop. A mesh compounds the difference in two
ways.

**Shared-medium hop penalty.** Nodes relaying on the same channel roughly halve
usable throughput per hop:

| Single-hop rate | 2 hops | 3 hops | 4 hops |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 Mbps (measured, 2.4 GHz close range) | 15 Mbps | 10 Mbps | 7.5 Mbps |
| 6.5 Mbps (HaLow 2 MHz, best case) | 3.3 Mbps | 2.2 Mbps | 1.6 Mbps |
| 2 Mbps (HaLow, realistic mid-range) | 1 Mbps | 0.7 Mbps | 0.5 Mbps |

A 2.4 GHz mesh four hops deep still carries video. A HaLow mesh four hops deep
is carrying position updates and text.

**Airtime.** The time to put a single 1500-byte frame on the air:

| Link | Rate | Airtime per 1500-byte frame |
|---|---|---|
| 2.4 GHz HT20, MCS7 | 65 Mbps | 0.18 ms |
| 2.4 GHz HT20, MCS0 | 6.5 Mbps | 1.85 ms |
| HaLow 2 MHz, MCS7 | 6.5 Mbps | 1.85 ms |
| HaLow 2 MHz, MCS0 | 0.65 Mbps | **18.5 ms** |
| HaLow 1 MHz, MCS0 | 0.30 Mbps | **40.0 ms** |

In a CSMA network, airtime is the currency. Every node that transmits blocks
every node that can hear it. At HaLow's long-range rates a single full-size frame
occupies the channel for 18 to 40 ms, and HaLow's 10× longer symbol time inflates
every preamble, ACK, and interframe space along with it. In a mesh carrying
position bursts from a dozen clients plus voice, that is the difference between a
network that feels immediate and one that feels congested.

## 8. Where HaLow Genuinely Wins, and by How Much

Everything above is the case against the assumption. This section is the case
for HaLow, because there is one and it deserves to be stated with numbers rather
than waved away.

900 MHz really does pass through vegetation and building materials better than
2.4 GHz. That is not the ground-reflection effect discussed in section 1, it is
absorption and scattering, and it is genuinely frequency-dependent. Using the
ITU vegetation model (`L = 0.2 * f^0.3 * R^0.6`, f in MHz, R in metres of
foliage depth):

| Foliage depth | 900 MHz loss | 2.4 GHz loss | HaLow advantage | Equivalent extra distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 m | 6.1 dB | 8.2 dB | 2.1 dB | ×1.13 |
| 20 m | 9.3 dB | 12.5 dB | 3.2 dB | ×1.20 |
| 50 m | 16.1 dB | 21.6 dB | 5.5 dB | ×1.37 |
| 100 m | 24.4 dB | 32.7 dB | 8.3 dB | ×1.62 |

Structures behave similarly. A typical exterior wall costs roughly 3–5 dB more
at 2.4 GHz than at 900 MHz, worth about ×1.19 to ×1.33 in equivalent distance.

**This is a real advantage. It is also much smaller than people expect.** Three
observations about the table:

1. The advantage is *relative*, not absolute. At 100 m of foliage depth 900 MHz
   is 8.3 dB better — but it is still eating 24.4 dB of loss. Heavy vegetation
   destroys both links. It destroys the 2.4 GHz link somewhat sooner.
2. Translated into range, the advantage is tens of percent, not multiples. The
   common mental model is that sub-GHz "goes through trees" while 2.4 GHz does
   not. What the numbers show is that both are badly attenuated and one is
   modestly less bad.
3. The penetration advantage and the noise-floor advantage do add together in
   the link budget — on a path through 50 m of canopy HaLow is ahead by about
   10 dB of noise floor plus 5.5 dB of foliage, roughly 15.5 dB total. But that
   is a single pool of margin, not two separate wins. Every dB spent punching
   through a treeline is a dB no longer available to extend range, and the extra
   margin is only redeemable at the low MCS rates where HaLow's throughput has
   already collapsed.

Our own obstructed-terrain testing shows what this looks like from the 2.4 GHz
side: with trees blocking line of sight, throughput dropped to about 1 Mbps by
300 m and the link was mostly gone by 400 m. A HaLow link in the same conditions
would plausibly have reached somewhere in the 400–500 m range instead — better,
worth having, and still nowhere near the "it will just work through the woods"
expectation the technology tends to carry.

The honest summary of HaLow's strengths: it is a good fit for low-rate telemetry
at range, non-line-of-sight sensor networks, and links that must survive
obstruction at any speed. Those are real applications. They are not the same
application as a mesh backbone that has to carry video, voice, and situational
awareness for a team.

## 9. Objections and Answers

The strongest case for HaLow does not rest on peak throughput, and a serious
proponent would not argue it there. It rests on **link reliability in non-ideal
conditions**. Below are the five best versions of that argument, stated as
forcefully as we can put them, each followed by our answer.

### Objection 1: The two-ray model assumes bare dirt. Real terrain has trees.

> Section 1's flat-earth analysis holds only over open ground with no vegetation
> or topography. In real terrain, a 33 cm wave diffracts around obstacles and
> penetrates foliage far better than a 12.5 cm wave. Introduce trees, buildings,
> or minor topography at 4 ft elevation and the 2.4 GHz link collapses below
> receiver sensitivity, at which point the 20 MHz pipe is worth nothing.

**This is the strongest objection on the list, and the first half of it is
correct.** The two-ray model does assume a clear path, real terrain is not
clear, and 900 MHz genuinely does better through vegetation and around edges.
We do not dispute any of that.

What we dispute is the magnitude. The advantage is frequently quoted as 10–12 dB
through moderate canopy. Run the ITU vegetation model and it is **5.5 dB through
50 m of foliage, 2.1 dB through 10 m** (section 8). Per-metre attenuation
figures quoted in isolation tend to inflate this, because vegetation loss is
sublinear in depth — it scales as roughly R^0.6, so a dB-per-metre number taken
from a thin-canopy measurement badly overestimates loss through a deep one.

Then follow the consequence through. Take 50 m of canopy, where HaLow enjoys
5.5 dB of foliage advantage on top of its 10 dB noise-floor advantage:

- 2.4 GHz absorbs 21.6 dB of foliage loss and drops several MCS steps. Our
  measured obstructed-terrain result is about 1 Mbps at 300 m, mostly gone by
  400 m.
- HaLow absorbs 16.1 dB and holds the link further — but it holds it at MCS0 or
  MCS1, which is 0.65 to 1.3 Mbps PHY, call it 0.3–0.6 Mbps of goodput.

So the honest scoreboard in the woods is: **a badly degraded 2.4 GHz link at
1–2 Mbps still delivers more than a healthy HaLow link at 0.3–0.6 Mbps.** HaLow
wins the coverage footprint. It loses the payload contest inside that footprint,
and it wins the footprint only in the region where what it is carrying has
already dropped to telemetry.

The real answer to trees is not 900 MHz. It is elevation, which buys 36 dB
(section 6) instead of 5.5 dB, and a LoRa fallback for the cases where the WiFi
link cannot survive at all.

### Objection 2: Sub-GHz is clean spectrum. 2.4 GHz is a sewer.

> The −96 dBm thermal noise floor for a 20 MHz channel is fiction in any real
> environment. 2.4 GHz is saturated with WiFi, Bluetooth, and industrial
> equipment, which lifts the effective noise floor well above thermal. Sub-1 GHz
> is comparatively empty, so HaLow actually achieves its theoretical sensitivity
> while 2.4 GHz does not.

A legitimate concern, and the one most often left unanswered. Three responses.

**First, this objection cuts against HaLow harder than it appears.** HaLow's
*entire* advantage in this document is the 10 dB thermal noise gap. That gap is
a thermal-only figure, and interference is what destroys it first. A 2 MHz
channel is also uniquely vulnerable: a single narrowband interferer landing
inside it takes the whole channel, while the same interferer inside a 20 MHz
OFDM channel damages a fraction of the subcarriers and the link degrades
gracefully rather than dropping.

**Second, 902–928 MHz is not empty.** It is 26 MHz of ISM spectrum shared with
smart meters, SCADA telemetry, industrial sensors, cordless devices, and a
rapidly growing population of LoRa deployments. The environments where 2.4 GHz
is genuinely crushed — dense urban and industrial — are precisely the
environments where 900 MHz is full of metering and telemetry traffic.

**Third, 2.4 GHz congestion is manageable in a way narrowband congestion is
not.** There are three non-overlapping 20 MHz channels, and our nodes ship a
channel-scan tool specifically so an operator can find the quietest one before
forming the mesh. More to the point, the deployment case for this equipment is
off-grid and austere — the field, the woods, the water — where 2.4 GHz is clean.
We are not proposing this as a downtown office network.

### Objection 3: At the link edge, 2.4 GHz throughput figures are meaningless.

> Showing 1–2 Mbps for 2.4 GHz at 1 km hides the fact that the link is sitting
> at its sensitivity threshold. That means retransmissions, latency spikes, and
> packet loss, which destroy real-time video protocols regardless of nominal
> bitrate. HaLow's 10 dB buffer delivers stable, low-jitter delivery even at a
> lower raw rate. Link uptime is not the same as usable delivery.

Half correct, and the half that is correct applies to both radios equally.

Yes, a link at its sensitivity threshold is a bad place to run real-time video —
for 2.4 GHz *and* for HaLow. The correct response is to plan video inside the
margin zone rather than at the cliff edge, which is what the implementation
margin in section 4 is for. Compare the two radios at the same point under the
same margin rule: at 1 km and 4 ft, received power is about −84.6 dBm on both
bands. That is 11.4 dB of SNR for 2.4 GHz, holding roughly 13 Mbps PHY, against
21.4 dB for HaLow, holding roughly 3.9 Mbps PHY. Same distance, same conditions,
same margin — and 2.4 GHz is carrying more than three times the payload while
sitting comfortably above its own cliff.

The jitter claim also inverts once the channel is loaded. HaLow's low-jitter
behaviour is measured with small, sparse packets. Offer a 2 MHz channel a
continuous bursty video payload and queueing delay climbs immediately, because
each full-size frame occupies the medium for 3 ms at MCS4 and 18.5 ms at MCS0
(section 7). Narrow channels do not absorb bursts, they serialise them.

And the symmetry from section 4 still applies: HaLow's video-capable sensitivity
(−81 dBm at MCS7) is *worse* than 2.4 GHz's floor (−88 dBm at MCS0). The
stability buffer is real, but it exists only at rates that cannot carry the video
it is supposed to be protecting.

### Objection 4: Modern codecs do not need the bandwidth.

> A useful tactical feed does not need 5–10 Mbps. H.265 or AV1 delivers an
> operational 720p15 or 480p30 stream at 300–500 kbps. HaLow carries that
> comfortably at 1 km, which makes 2.4 GHz's extra bandwidth redundant for a
> single-camera application.

Two problems, and the second is fatal.

**The codec claim assumes a camera that is not moving.** Sub-500 kbps encoding
depends on long groups of pictures and heavy temporal prediction: very few
intra-coded frames, most bits spent on inter-frame differences. That works well
for a fixed camera watching a static scene. A body-worn camera, a vehicle mount,
or a mast in wind produces constant global motion, temporal prediction stops
predicting, and the encoder is forced into I-frame bursts. At a 300–500 kbps
ceiling those bursts either blow the budget or get quantised into macroblock mush
at exactly the moment something is happening. Realistic headroom for a moving
tactical camera is closer to 1.5–3 Mbps.

**More importantly, the geometry does not work out.** At 1 km, HaLow does carry
500 kbps comfortably — but so does 2.4 GHz, at roughly 13 Mbps PHY, and that is
inside the range where both radios work. HaLow's *exclusive* zone, the region
past roughly 1.2 km where 2.4 GHz has dropped out and HaLow has not, is MCS0
territory: 0.65 Mbps PHY, about 0.3–0.4 Mbps of goodput, shared with every other
node on the channel. That is at or below the codec floor before a single I-frame
burst, and it is halved again at every mesh hop. **HaLow's video-capable range
ends at approximately the same place 2.4 GHz's does.** Where HaLow keeps going,
it is not carrying video either.

### Objection 5: Wide channels burn power and generate heat.

> Running a 20 MHz channel at high power on a body-worn node drains batteries and
> produces heat. HaLow achieves comparable range at lower consumption because of
> the narrow channel.

This one does not survive contact with the hardware.

DC draw and heat in a radio like this are dominated by the power amplifier, not
by channel width. Both radios in this comparison run 27 dBm (500 mW) conducted;
at typical PA efficiency that is roughly 2 W of DC draw **on either band**. The
difference in baseband processing between a 20 MHz FFT and a 2 MHz FFT is a
rounding error next to that. A complete Nucleus node measures about 3 W in normal
operation, power amplifier included.

There is a real power story in HaLow, but it is not this one. HaLow's efficiency
features — target wake time, scheduled sleep, restricted access windows — are
designed for sensors that wake, send a short burst, and sleep for minutes or
hours. **A mesh relay cannot sleep.** It has to listen continuously in order to
forward traffic for other nodes. The architectural advantage that makes HaLow
excellent for battery-powered sensor fleets is structurally unavailable to a mesh
backbone, which is the application under discussion here.

### What the objections add up to

The strongest case for HaLow is that it keeps a link alive in conditions where
2.4 GHz drops. That is true, and section 8 quantifies it honestly. The case fails
on what the surviving link is worth. In every regime where HaLow is connected and
2.4 GHz is not, HaLow is running at 0.3–0.65 Mbps PHY, which is a telemetry pipe.
In every regime where both are connected, 2.4 GHz delivers three to ten times
more. There is no operating point in this comparison where HaLow carries more
useful traffic than 2.4 GHz — only points where it carries a little, and 2.4 GHz
carries nothing.

That is a real and useful property. It is also the exact job we assigned to LoRa,
which does it at 12 km instead of 2 km.

## 10. Conclusion

Laid side by side, on identical transmit hardware, at the antenna heights people
actually operate at:

| | 2.4 GHz HT20 | HaLow 2 MHz |
|---|---|---|
| Propagation advantage past 50 m at 4 ft | — | **0 dB** |
| Noise floor advantage | — | 10 dB |
| Peak throughput | ~30 Mbps measured | ~4 Mbps realistic |
| Range at equal PHY rate (6.5 Mbps) | 1,219 m | 815 m |
| Range at minimum rate | 1,219 m @ 6.5 Mbps | 2,168 m @ 0.65 Mbps |
| Four-hop mesh throughput | 7.5 Mbps | ~0.5–1.6 Mbps |
| Airtime per frame at long range | 1.85 ms | 18.5 ms |
| Foliage/structure penetration | — | 2–8 dB better |
| Gain from putting one node 10 m up | **36 dB / ~8× range** | 36 dB / ~8× range |

HaLow buys roughly 1.8× more distance-to-last-gasp in exchange for giving up
about 90% of the throughput, and it gives up its speed advantage well before
2.4 GHz gives up its link. In obstructed terrain it holds a modest and real
advantage of a few dB. Everywhere else in this comparison, the sub-GHz
propagation benefit that motivates the whole argument is mathematically absent at
the heights a portable node is used at.

We built the mesh on 2.4 GHz because the mission is carrying real network traffic
— ATAK situational awareness, voice, video, file transfer — between nodes, and
because the honest answer to "how do I get more range" is not a different band.
It is getting one node higher, with LoRa underneath as the fallback for when the
WiFi link cannot reach at all. That combination gives a high ceiling where the
network is usable and a long tail where it is not, which is exactly the shape of
the problem.
